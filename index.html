    Guide

    The complete guide to using an online calculator

    Order of operations, scientific functions, angle units, percentages, number bases and keyboard shortcuts, explained in plain language with examples you can try above.

    A calculator looks simple, but getting reliable answers depends on understanding a handful of ideas: the order in which operations are performed, what each function key really does, how angles are measured, and how rounding affects what you see on screen. This guide walks through all of it using the same Standard, Scientific, Percentage and Programmer modes available in the AuraCalc workspace at the top of this page. Whether you are checking homework, pricing a sale, converting a hex colour or working through an engineering formula, you will find worked examples that you can type in right now and compare against the result.

    Order of operations: PEMDAS and BODMAS explained

    When an expression contains more than one operation, a calculator cannot simply work from left to right. Mathematicians agreed on a fixed order of operations so that every expression has exactly one correct value. In the United States the order is taught as PEMDAS (Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication and Division, Addition and Subtraction). In the United Kingdom, India and many other countries it is BODMAS (Brackets, Orders, Division and Multiplication, Addition and Subtraction). The two mnemonics describe exactly the same rules.

    The four levels of priority

    1. Parentheses first. Anything inside brackets is evaluated before the rest of the expression. Nested brackets are resolved from the innermost pair outward.
    2. Exponents and roots next. Powers such as x², x³ and xʸ, and roots such as √x and ∛x, bind more tightly than multiplication.
    3. Multiplication and division share one level and are performed from left to right.
    4. Addition and subtraction share the final level and are also performed from left to right.

    Negative numbers and exponents

    The expression −3² equals −9, because the exponent is applied before the minus sign. If you want the square of negative three, write (−3)² and the answer is 9. AuraCalc follows the same convention that textbooks and spreadsheet programs use, so typing −3² gives −9 while (−3)² gives 9.

    Implicit multiplication and the famous 6 ÷ 2(1 + 2) puzzle

    Viral math puzzles often hinge on implicit multiplication, where a number sits directly next to a bracket. Different calculators disagree about it. AuraCalc treats implicit multiplication exactly like ordinary multiplication and works from left to right, so 6 ÷ 2(1 + 2) evaluates to 9. If you meant to divide by the whole product, add explicit brackets: 6 ÷ (2 × (1 + 2)) equals 1. Whenever an expression looks ambiguous, brackets are the cheapest insurance against a wrong answer.

    Standard calculator basics and memory keys

    Standard mode covers everyday arithmetic: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, percentages and decimals. AC clears everything, ⌫ deletes the last character, +/− flips the sign of the number you are typing, and = finishes the calculation and stores it on the history tape. While you type, the large display shows a live preview of the running answer, so you can see the result before you press equals.

    Memory keys explained: MC, MR, M+, M− and MS

    The memory keys give you a scratch pad that survives between calculations, which is ideal for adding up several separate totals.

    • MS (memory store) replaces the memory with the current value.
    • M+ adds the current value to the number in memory.
    • M− subtracts the current value from the number in memory.
    • MR (memory recall) inserts the stored number into your current calculation.
    • MC (memory clear) sets memory back to zero.

    Imagine you are totalling three receipts of 24.50, 18.75 and 9.99. Type 24.5 and press M+, type 18.75 and press M+, then 9.99 and M+. Press MR and the display shows 53.24. If one purchase is refunded, type 5 and press M− to bring the stored total down to 48.24. A small M badge on the display tells you whenever memory is holding a non-zero value, and the stored number stays until you press MC or reload the page.

    How to use scientific calculator functions

    Scientific mode adds a function grid above the standard keypad. Function keys open a bracket automatically, so pressing sin followed by 30 and ) builds sin(30). If you press a function key right after finishing a calculation, the previous answer is wrapped inside the function, which makes it easy to chain operations.

    Trigonometry: sin, cos and tan

    Sine, cosine and tangent describe the relationships between the sides of a right-angled triangle. Sine is the opposite side divided by the hypotenuse, cosine is the adjacent side divided by the hypotenuse, and tangent is the opposite side divided by the adjacent side. They also describe waves, rotation and oscillation, which is why they appear in physics, engineering, music and computer graphics. The table below lists values you can verify in DEG mode.

    Inverse trigonometry: asin, acos and atan

    The inverse functions work backwards: you give them a ratio and they return the angle. In DEG mode, asin(0.5) returns 30 and atan(1) returns 45. Sine and cosine only produce values between −1 and 1, so asking for asin(2) or acos(−3) triggers an Invalid Domain message.

    Logarithms: log and ln

    A logarithm answers the question “what power produces this number?”. The log key uses base 10, so log(1000) is 3 and log(100) is 2. The ln key uses Euler's number e (about 2.71828) as its base, which makes ln(e) equal to 1. Logarithms are only defined for positive numbers, so log(0) and ln(−5) are invalid. To find a logarithm in any other base, divide two logarithms: log base 2 of 8 is ln(8) ÷ ln(2), which equals 3.

    Exponents and roots: x², x³, xʸ, eˣ, 10ˣ, √x and ∛x

    The x² and x³ keys square and cube the number before them. The xʸ key raises any base to any power, so 2 xʸ 10 equals 1,024. The eˣ and 10ˣ keys are shortcuts for powers of e and 10, which are common in growth, decay and scientific notation: 10ˣ with x = 3 gives 1,000. Roots undo powers. √144 equals 12, ∛27 equals 3, and the cube root works for negative numbers too, so ∛(−8) equals −2. A square root of a negative number has no real answer and returns Invalid Domain.

    Factorials, constants and absolute value

    The factorial n! multiplies all whole numbers from 1 up to n. For example, 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120, and by definition 0! = 1. Factorials count arrangements and appear in combinatorics: the number of ways to choose 3 items from 10 is 10! ÷ (3! × 7!), which equals 120. Factorial is defined only for non-negative whole numbers, and the result grows so fast that anything above 170! overflows the range of standard floating-point numbers, in which case AuraCalc shows Overflow.

    The π and e keys insert high-precision constants. The area of a circle with radius 5 is π × 5², or about 78.5398. The |x| key returns the absolute value, which is the distance of a number from zero, so |−7.5| equals 7.5.

    Understanding error messages

    • Division by Zero appears for operations such as 5 ÷ 0 or 0^(−1).
    • Invalid Domain means the input is outside what a function accepts, for example √(−1), log(0) or asin(2).
    • Syntax Error means the expression cannot be parsed, such as unmatched or empty brackets.
    • Overflow means the result is too large to represent.

    Degrees vs radians explained simply

    Degrees and radians are two units for the same thing: how far you have turned. A degree divides a full circle into 360 equal slices, a convention that dates back to ancient Babylonian mathematics. A radian is defined by the circle itself: one radian is the angle that creates an arc exactly as long as the circle's radius. Because the circumference of a circle is 2π times its radius, a full turn is 2π radians, and a half turn is π radians.

    To convert degrees to radians, multiply by π ÷ 180. To convert radians to degrees, multiply by 180 ÷ π. One radian is therefore about 57.2958°, and one degree is about 0.0174533 radians.

    Everyday percentage and discount formulas

    Percentages describe a part out of one hundred, and they show up in shopping, tipping, interest, grades and statistics. Percentage mode in AuraCalc has four filters that match the most common questions, each showing the formula and a step-by-step breakdown.

    What is X% of Y?

    Multiply the value by the percentage and divide by 100: Y × X ÷ 100. So 15% of 80 is 80 × 15 ÷ 100 = 12. A handy mental shortcut is to find 10% by moving the decimal point one place, then build up from there.

    X is what percent of Y?

    Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: X ÷ Y × 100. If you scored 12 out of 80, the percentage is 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = 15%.

    Percentage increase and decrease

    Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value, and multiply by 100: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Going from 80 to 100 is a 25% increase. Going back from 100 to 80 is a 20% decrease, not 25%, because the percentage is always measured against the starting value. This asymmetry explains why a 25% rise followed by a 25% fall leaves you below where you began: 100 becomes 125, then 93.75.

    Discounts, sales tax and final price

    Take the discount first, then add tax to the discounted price: price × (1 − discount) × (1 + tax). A 120 item at 25% off costs 90, and adding 8% tax brings the final price to 97.20. Stacked discounts do not simply add up. A 20% discount followed by an extra 10% off is 1 − 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.28, a 28% total saving rather than 30%.

    Reverse percentages, margins and percentage points

    To find an original price from a sale price, divide by the remaining fraction. If an item costs 64 after a 20% discount, the original price was 64 ÷ 0.8 = 80. Business owners should also separate markup from margin. With a cost of 60 and a selling price of 100, the markup is (100 − 60) ÷ 60 = 66.7%, while the margin is (100 − 60) ÷ 100 = 40%. Finally, remember that percentage points and percent are different things: moving from 10% to 12% is a rise of 2 percentage points, but a 20% relative increase.

    Using the % key in Standard mode

    In Standard and Scientific modes the % key follows the familiar pocket-calculator behaviour. Typing 200 + 10 % adds ten percent of 200 and returns 220, 200 − 15 % returns 170, and 50 × 10 % returns 5. On its own, 50 % is simply 0.5.

    Binary, hexadecimal and octal numbers

    People count in base 10 because we have ten fingers, but computers work in base 2 because electronic switches have two states. A positional number system assigns each digit a weight that is a power of the base. In decimal the weights are 1, 10, 100 and so on. In binary they are 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on. Hexadecimal (base 16) adds the letters A to F for the values 10 to 15, and octal (base 8) uses the digits 0 to 7. Programmer mode shows any value in all four bases at once.

    Converting by hand

    To convert hexadecimal FF to decimal, multiply each digit by its weight: 15 × 16 + 15 = 255. To convert decimal 45 to binary, break it into powers of two: 32 + 8 + 4 + 1, which is 101101. Hexadecimal is popular with programmers because each hex digit maps to exactly four bits, so 0xB7 is simply 1011 0111 in binary. In Programmer mode you can skip the arithmetic: type a number in the active base and read the other three bases instantly, or switch base to convert the value you are working on.

    Bitwise operations: AND, OR, XOR, NOT and shifts

    Bitwise operators work on the individual bits of whole numbers. Using 12 (binary 1100) and 10 (binary 1010):

    • 12 AND 10 keeps bits that are set in both numbers and gives 8 (1000).
    • 12 OR 10 keeps bits set in either number and gives 14 (1110).
    • 12 XOR 10 keeps bits set in exactly one number and gives 6 (0110).
    • NOT 5 flips every bit and gives −6 in two's complement arithmetic.
    • 1 << 4 shifts bits left and gives 16, while 256 >> 2 shifts right and gives 64.

    Masks are a classic use case. FF AND 0F in hexadecimal returns 0F, which isolates the low four bits of a byte.

    Word size, wrap-around and integer division

    Real processors store integers in a fixed number of bits. The word size filter lets you choose 8, 16, 32 or 64 bits. Results that do not fit wrap around using two's complement, exactly as they would in hardware, so in 8-bit mode 127 + 1 becomes −128. Negative values are displayed in HEX, OCT and BIN as their unsigned bit patterns. Division in Programmer mode is whole-number division that truncates toward zero, so 7 ÷ 2 equals 3, and the MOD key returns the remainder, so 7 MOD 2 equals 1.

    Where number bases show up in real life

    • Web colours: #FF8800 means red 255, green 136 (hex 88) and blue 0.
    • File permissions: the Unix permission 755 is octal, which expands to the bit groups 111 101 101.
    • Networking: the subnet octet 255 is 11111111 in binary, eight bits all set.

    Rounding, precision and floating-point numbers

    Computers store most decimal fractions as binary approximations. In many programming languages, 0.1 + 0.2 produces 0.30000000000000004 because neither 0.1 nor 0.2 can be written exactly in binary. AuraCalc rounds its working results to 15 significant digits, which removes this noise from everyday sums while still being far more precise than you will need for most tasks.

    The Rounding filter controls how a finished answer is displayed and copied:

    • Auto / Exact shows up to 15 significant digits and switches to scientific notation for extremely large or small values.
    • 2 Decimals is ideal for money.
    • 4 and 6 Decimals suit measurements, rates and scientific work.
    • Scientific Notation (E) shows values such as 6.022E+23, handy for physics and chemistry.

    These filters change only the displayed text. The underlying full-precision number is still used if you continue calculating, so choosing 2 Decimals will not introduce compounding rounding errors. The Thousands separator switch adds commas for readability, for example 1,250,000.50. Turn it off before copying results into a spreadsheet, script or database, where 1250000.50 is safer to paste.

    Putting it all together

    The fastest way to get reliable answers is to build a few habits. Choose the mode that matches the problem, check whether the angle unit is DEG or RAD before any trigonometry, add brackets whenever an expression could be read two ways, and use the live preview to catch typos before you press equals. Star the calculations you rely on so they are one click away on the history tape, and export the tape as a TXT or CSV file when you need a record of your working. With those habits, a free browser calculator can handle everything from a quick tip to a university physics problem.

    FAQ

    Frequently asked questions

    Quick answers about shortcuts, functions, angle modes, privacy and precision.

    What keyboard shortcuts does the calculator support?

    Type digits 0–9, a decimal point, + − * / for arithmetic, ( and ) for brackets, ^ for powers, ! for factorial and % for percent. Press Enter or = to calculate, Backspace to delete the last character and Escape to clear everything. In Scientific mode you can also press P for π, E for Euler's number, S, C and T for sin, cos and tan, L for log, N for ln and R for square root.

    Which scientific functions are included?

    Scientific mode includes sin, cos, tan, asin, acos and atan, base-10 and natural logarithms, squares, cubes, any power, eˣ and 10ˣ, square and cube roots, factorial, absolute value, the constants π and e, a 1/x reciprocal key, an Ans key and nested parentheses.

    What is the difference between DEG and RAD?

    DEG measures angles in degrees, where a full circle is 360°, while RAD measures them in radians, where a full circle is 2π. Use DEG for everyday geometry and RAD for calculus and physics. For example, sin(30) equals 0.5 in DEG mode but about −0.988 in RAD mode.

    Is my calculation history private?

    Yes. The history tape is stored only in your own browser using localStorage. Nothing is uploaded to a server, and you can delete single entries or press Clear History at any time. Clearing your browser data also removes it.

    How do I change decimal rounding and precision?

    Use the Rounding filter above the display to choose Auto / Exact, 2, 4 or 6 decimals, or scientific notation. Rounding only changes how the answer is shown and copied; the full-precision value is still used if you keep calculating. In Auto mode results show up to 15 significant digits.

    Does the calculator work on mobile phones and tablets?

    Yes. The layout is responsive, the keys are large enough for thumbs, and the history tape becomes a collapsible drawer on small screens. Because all calculations run locally in your browser, it stays fast even on slow connections.

    How do I calculate percentages and discounts?

    Switch to Percentage mode and pick a filter: what is X% of Y, X is what % of Y, percentage increase or decrease, or final price after discount and tax. Enter your numbers and the answer, formula and breakdown update instantly. In Standard mode you can also type 200 + 10 % to add ten percent to 200.

    What do MC, MR, M+, M− and MS do?

    MS stores the current value in memory, M+ adds it to the stored value and M− subtracts it. MR recalls the stored number into your calculation and MC clears memory. An M badge appears on the display while memory holds a non-zero value.

    How does Programmer mode convert between number bases?

    Choose DEC, HEX, OCT or BIN and the display shows the current value in all four bases at once. Type in the active base, switch bases at any time to convert, and use AND, OR, XOR, NOT, shifts and MOD on whole numbers within the 8, 16, 32 or 64-bit word size you select.

    Is the calculator free, and is it accurate?

    It is completely free, with no sign-up or install. The expression parser follows the standard order of operations (PEMDAS/BODMAS), avoids unsafe eval(), and reports clear errors such as Division by Zero, Invalid Domain and Syntax Error instead of guessing.
